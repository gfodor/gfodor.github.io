The Split Key

An integer comparison, made to do work no one wrote it to do — and a quarter million voxels collapse into a few hundred quads in a few milliseconds.

Source: webspace-sdk/webspace-engine · src/terra/ · master @ 2026-05-24 · MPL-2.0

Premise§ 1

The webspace engine renders three-dimensional worlds in a browser. The terrain is voxels: a sixty-four-by-sixty-four-by-sixty-four cube of opaque blocks per chunk, 262,144 of them. The renderer cannot draw 262,144 individual faces at sixty frames per second. It draws a few hundred quads — large rectangular patches of identically-shaded surface, fused along axis-aligned planes. The function that decides which voxels to fuse, and into what quads, takes about a millisecond per chunk.

This is its trick.

The cast§ 2

We will follow one chunk. The chunk at world index (cx = 0, cz = 0), generated by the islands generator with seed = 42. Inside this chunk, on the horizontal plane y = 10, there sits a five-by-three rectangle of grass-colored voxels at coordinates (x = 4..8, z = 12..14). All fifteen voxels are the same shade of green: r=78, g=164, b=72.

To their right, another patch — same fifteen voxels, same grass type, but each one a slightly different shade because the palette is noise-driven and the noise field varies. By the end of this reading, the first patch will become exactly one quad on the GPU. The second will become fifteen. The reason will turn out to be a single line of source code.

From noise to height§ 3

Before the mesher sees anything, the chunk has to be filled. The islands generator walks every column (x, z) and asks how tall the dirt should be. The function it calls is getTerrainHeight, and the noise it consults is a simplex sample drawn from four numbers derived from (x, z) — the cosines and sines of each coordinate mapped onto the world's 512-meter wrap.

The four-number business is the world's other trick, and not the one this vignette is about. It is the parameterization of a two-torus into four dimensions — two cos/sin pairs, one per wrapped axis — which is why the terrain has no visible seams where the world wraps onto itself. A future vignette can take that as its own throughline.

For our purposes only one thing matters: for the column at (x = 6, z = 13), getTerrainHeight returns 11. So the dirt fills the column up to y = 10. The voxel at (6, 10, 13) is the topmost grass block. The voxel at (6, 11, 13) is air. There is a boundary, axis-aligned, between them. The mesher's job is to find that boundary, decide how large a rectangle it can be described by in a single quad, and emit it.

From height to voxels§ 4

The chunk's storage is one Uint8Array of length 64 × 64 × 64 × 8 — a little over two megabytes. Eight bytes per voxel: type, r, g, b, plus a low-LOD color triple, plus a palette index. The grass at (6, 10, 13) reads as bytes [4, 78, 164, 72, …]. The air above it has type byte 0. Everything you need to mesh this chunk — including the color a quad will be — already lives in these bytes.

The boundary problem§ 5

A quad must be flat. It must lie in a single plane. Greedy meshing exploits this by considering each of the three axes in turn and, within each axis, each slice perpendicular to it. For the top faces of grass we care about, the relevant slice is y = 10 looking up. The mesher's question, for every cell on that sixty-four-by-sixty-four plane, is the same: does the voxel below have a face pointing up — that is, is there opaque voxel here and air above?

The mask§ 6

The first half of the algorithm answers that question for every cell on the slice and stows the answer into a pair of arrays. mask is a vector of integers, one per cell, recording whether there is a face here. vals is a parallel vector recording what kind of face it is. The mesher fills both in a single pass over the slice.

Watch this carefully. The mask is binary. The values are integers. The sweep below will compare those integer values pairwise: two cells can merge into a single quad if, and only if, they have the same value in vals. So the question of which voxels merge has been moved upstream into the question of what value each cell stores. The vignette is about to make a great deal out of what is inside vals[n], and the loop that fills it gives nothing away.

The sweep§ 7

The second half is the lexicographic sweep. Walk the slice row by row, column by column. When a cell with mask[n] set is found, freeze its value as cv and grow a rectangle: extend w along the horizontal axis while adjacent cells have mask set and value equal to cv. Then extend h downward for as long as every cell in the next row, across that width, also matches. Emit the rectangle. Zero out the mask under it. Advance past the rectangle's right edge and continue.

For our grass patch at (x = 4..8, z = 12..14), this is the moment fifteen voxels become one quad. The sweep arrives at (i = 4, j = 12). mask[n] is true; vals[n] is some integer K. Growing width: cells (5, 12), (6, 12), (7, 12), (8, 12) all have mask set and vals equal to K. Cell (9, 12) does not match — different value. So w = 5. Growing height: row j = 13 matches across width five; row j = 14 matches; row j = 15 does not. So h = 3. The mesher emits one quad: five wide, three tall, at the right plane. It zeroes fifteen mask entries. It advances i by five. It continues.

The split key§ 8 · climax

What the sweep just did is worth pausing on. The value the mask cell carried — the integer the inner loop compared against — was not a boolean dressed up in an Int32Array. It was packed. The voxel type sat in the low byte. Red, then green, then blue, occupied the three bytes above it. A single 32-bit integer, written once by a small function in chunk.js the mesher never reads:

The mesher does not know it is doing color-aware merging. It is comparing 32-bit integers in a tight inner loop, and the choice of what to pack into those integers is the entire algorithm.

So when the inner loop asks whether the cell to the right is “the same” — cv === vals[n + w] — it is asking a single integer equality. Two cells match if and only if they have the same orientation, the same material type, and exactly the same color. The patch of differently-shaded greens next door fragments into fifteen quads. The uniform patch we have been following coalesces into one. The same loop, with no special case for color, does both.

The line of source code that does this work is short enough to fit on a single line and dense enough that a casual reader will read past it without noticing what just happened. chunk.voxels[voxel] | (r << 8) | (g << 16) | (b << 24). Four bit-shifts and three OR operations. The mesher's claim to color awareness is a fiction maintained by the caller. The algorithm is a packed-integer comparison; everything else is bookkeeping.

Aftermath§ 11

The quad list — for our chunk, a few hundred entries — flows through a protocol-buffer encode, gets stored under the key "1/0/42/0/0/1" in IndexedDB so the next page load skips the noise sampling entirely, and arrives in the main thread, where an instanced Terrain mesh draws every chunk's quads in one call per material. A million voxels' worth of terrain renders at sixty frames per second on a laptop. None of that is interesting. The interesting part — the part that justifies a vignette — happened in chunk.js, line 160, with an integer.

In summary · review queue · § 12

Greedy meshing in webspace-engine collapses a chunk of voxels into a small number of quads by sweeping each axis, building a per-slice mask whose value at each cell is a 32-bit integer packing both face type and color, and emitting rectangles wherever adjacent cells have matching values. The mesher does not know about color. The equality test does the work. Two patches of the same type but different shades fragment into many quads. A uniform patch becomes one. This is why a webspace's terrain renders at sixty frames per second despite carrying a million voxels' worth of geometry per visible region. The cleverness lives at the boundary between two files: the caller decides what to pack; the mesher decides what to merge; the bytes do both jobs at once.
